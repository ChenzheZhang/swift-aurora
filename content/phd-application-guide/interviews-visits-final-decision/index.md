Interviews and visits are not only about being evaluated. They are also opportunities to learn whether a program is a good environment for the next several years of training.

## Interview Preparation

For interviews, I would prepare concise answers to a few recurring questions:

- What are your current research interests?
- What research experience best prepared you for a PhD?
- What methods, data work, or writing experience can you discuss in detail?
- Why does this program fit your interests?
- What kinds of questions might you want to work on in graduate school?

It helps to practice explaining a research project at different levels of detail. A short version should communicate the question, method, data, and contribution. A longer version should be ready for follow-up questions about identification, limitations, and next steps.

## Campus Visits

Campus visits are valuable because they show how the department works beyond the website. I would use visits to learn about advising, coursework, seminars, research groups, student support, and placement preparation.

Good questions for current graduate students include:

- How do students usually find advisers?
- How often do students meet with advisers after coursework?
- What seminars or workshops are most useful for applied research?
- How collaborative is the graduate-student environment?
- How transparent are expectations around funding, teaching, research assistant work, and milestones?
- What is the realistic living cost in the area?

I would also pay attention to whether students seem comfortable speaking honestly. A department can look strong on paper, but the day-to-day advising and peer environment still matter.

## Evaluating the Final Choice

My final decision combined research fit, advising style, placement, living cost, funding clarity, department culture, and personal constraints. I do not publish detailed stipend numbers, but I do think applicants should ask enough questions to understand whether the package is financially workable.

For applied, agricultural, environmental, energy, resource, and development-oriented applicants, the strongest choice may be the program where the field environment is active, advising options are realistic, and the training supports the kind of empirical work the applicant wants to do.

The final choice is partly analytical and partly personal. A spreadsheet can organize the decision, but it cannot fully replace conversations with faculty, current students, mentors, and people who understand your goals.
