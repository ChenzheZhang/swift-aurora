The PhD application process is easier to manage when it is treated as a multi-month project rather than a short writing sprint. The exact calendar differs across fields and countries, but the broad structure below is generalizable for many U.S. economics and applied economics programs.

## September: School List and Faculty-Fit Research

September is a good time to build the first version of the school list. I would start by separating programs into broad categories: Economics PhD, Applied Economics PhD, Agricultural Economics PhD, and Environmental/Resource Economics programs. The boundaries are not always clean, so the goal is to understand where your research interests can be supported rather than to classify departments perfectly.

Useful tasks include:

- Build a spreadsheet of programs, deadlines, application requirements, and broad research areas.
- Read department websites, placement pages, and recent working papers.
- Identify whether the program has multiple faculty members working in areas close to your interests.
- Note whether the application requires special forms, course descriptions, writing samples, or separate fellowship materials.

## October-November: SOP, CV, Writing Sample, and Recommendation Letters

October and November are the core preparation months. The statement of purpose, CV, writing sample, and recommendation letters should tell a coherent story about research preparation and future fit.

For each program, I would keep a short note on why the program fits my interests. These notes can later become customized paragraphs in the SOP. The goal is not to name as many people as possible; it is to explain why the intellectual environment fits your research direction.

This is also the period to confirm recommendation writers, share materials with them, and give them enough context to write specific letters. A specific and strong letter is usually more useful than a generic letter from a famous professor.

## December-January: Application Submission

Most applications are submitted in December and January. This stage is administrative but high-stakes, because small differences across portals can create avoidable errors.

Practical habits that helped:

- Track every deadline in one spreadsheet.
- Record whether transcripts, test scores, writing samples, course descriptions, and recommendation letters have been submitted.
- Submit before the deadline when possible, especially for portals with multiple steps.
- Save final PDFs of submitted SOPs and CVs for your own records.

## January-March: Interviews, Visits, and Waiting

After submission, the process becomes less predictable. Some programs interview, some invite admitted students to visits, and some communicate mainly through formal decisions. Waiting is part of the cycle, so it helps to prepare questions before invitations arrive.

During this period, I would prepare concise answers about research interests, current projects, methods background, and why a particular program fits. I would also keep a list of questions for current graduate students and faculty about advising, coursework, seminars, placement support, and day-to-day department culture.

## April 15: Final Decision Deadline

For many U.S. programs, April 15 is the final decision deadline. By this point, the decision should combine research fit, advising style, funding clarity, placement, department culture, location, and living cost.

The best choice is not always the program with the broadest name recognition. For applicants in applied, agricultural, environmental, energy, resource, or development fields, a program with strong field fit and supportive advising can be an excellent environment for graduate training.
